# Shift-Aware Commute Checks Fit Michigan's Evidence ( Fable 5.2 Research )

Getting to work without a car in Metro Detroit is the best-documented, most concentrated and most buildable mobility problem in Michigan as of November 2026, and the team's front-running idea sits directly on it. About **21% of Detroit households have no vehicle** (three times the state rate), **36% of Detroiters are transportation insecure**, and the regional transit authority's own study finds Detroiters can reach only **13% of tri-county jobs within a 60-minute peak transit trip**, with access to major employment clusters falling by about half after 11 p.m. Other Michigan problems are large (senior and disability mobility, Medicaid medical rides, insurance costs, road condition, EV charging, supplier job losses), but each is either already crowded with state-funded tools, lacks open data, or cannot be demonstrated in a one-day build. The round-trip, shift-specific commute check is supported by the evidence and is differentiated from the Transit app and Google Maps, with two conditions: the word "reliably" must be backed by a transparent risk score, because live bus data is not obtainable in a day, and the tool must offer a fallback when the answer is "no", because a verdict alone does not get anyone to work. Open GTFS feeds for DDOT and SMART, the Google Routes API, and IBM's tool-calling stack cover the build. Several popular statistics (the Brookings job-access rankings, "most dangerous city for pedestrians", "one in four Michiganders has a disability") are old, contradicted or unverified and should stay out of the pitch.

## One in five Detroit households has no car, and the jobs sit across the city line

The affected group is specific and countable. Census figures reported in November 2025 put car-less households at 21% in Detroit against 7% statewide and 8% nationally, and University of Michigan researchers classify 36% of Detroit residents as transportation insecure, about double the 17% national rate; among Detroiters without a car the share is 71%, and among households earning under $30,000 it is 56% ([Planet Detroit](https://planetdetroit.org/2025/11/transportation-insecurity-detroit-research/)). The Regional Transit Authority (RTA) counts roughly **76,000 no-vehicle households in the DDOT service area**, and finds another 27% of households own fewer cars than they have workers ([RTA Workforce Mobility Equity Report](https://www.rtamichigan.org/app/uploads/2025/06/RTA-Detroit-Workforce-Mobility-Equity-Report-F-1.pdf)). Car ownership is not a clean line either: 27% of Detroiters who do own a car are still transportation insecure, so "no car" understates the population with a commute problem ([Planet Detroit](https://planetdetroit.org/2025/11/transportation-insecurity-detroit-research/)).

The mismatch is structural. Nearly 70% of employed Detroit residents work outside the city ([Axios Detroit](https://www.axios.com/local/detroit/2025/09/29/detroit-residents-commuter-wage-gap)), and the RTA study counted 50,781 Detroiters in lower-wage jobs outside the city against 15,377 inside it (2018 data). That same study is the single strongest piece of evidence for the team's idea: within 60 minutes at peak, transit reaches about 21% of entry-level ("minimal-preparedness") jobs in the tri-county area and 13% of all jobs; **27 of 40 high-need Detroit census tracts cannot reach any suburban job cluster**; and at 11 p.m. clusters such as Stellantis Jefferson North, Amazon Fairgrounds, Cadillac Stamping and Ascension St. John lose about half their transit access, with only three suburban clusters served around the clock ([RTA report](https://www.rtamichigan.org/app/uploads/2025/06/RTA-Detroit-Workforce-Mobility-Equity-Report-F-1.pdf)). That overnight cliff is precisely the second-shift failure the idea targets.

Reliability compounds coverage. DDOT's own dashboard for January 2026, the latest month available when the notes were compiled, shows system on-time performance of 68% in the morning and 62% in the afternoon, and only 148 of 184 scheduled morning buses leaving the garage ([DDOT Performance Dashboard](https://detroitmi.gov/taxonomy/term/5641)); the agency's director puts the goal at 85% ([WDET](https://wdet.org/2026/01/28/transit-director-says-ddot-adding-buses-working-on-efficiency/)). SMART finished 2024 at nearly 68% on time ([TRU State of Transit 2025](https://www.detroittransit.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/State-of-Transit-Report-2025-WEB.pdf)). A two-seat DDOT-to-SMART commute therefore carries real odds of a missed connection, and thin headways turn a miss into a long delay: one DDOT route added weekend service in June 2026 at a 70-minute frequency ([DDOT June 2026 service changes](https://detroitmi.gov/sites/detroitmi.localhost/files/2026-06/June%202026%20Public%20Hearing%20for%20Proposed%20Service%20Changes.pdf)). Underneath sits a funding gap: the RTA calculates $97 per capita in regional transit funding for FY2023 against a peer average of $468 ([RTA Funding Overview](https://www.rtamichigan.org/app/uploads/2025/10/AppC_Funding-Overview-Tech-Memo_508_10102025.pdf)).

The timing is unusually good for a pitch. Wayne County voters ended the suburban opt-out system on August 4, 2026 with 67% support ([Transportation Riders United](https://www.detroittransit.org/?p=21965)), and SMART plans eight new routes and five extensions phased from September 2026 through 2029, with Canton, Livonia, Plymouth and Northville rated best for fixed routes ([Detroit News via Mass Transit](https://www.masstransitmag.com/management/news/55403322/mi-smart-plans-for-expansion-into-new-communities-after-countywide-vote-passes)). The service map is changing every few months, which means the answer to "can I get to this plant?" will change too, and a tool reading the published schedules stays current without anyone maintaining it. The human story is current as well: Deanne Austin, a Detroiter who does not drive, spent eight years taking three buses each way to a Livonia manufacturing job and told Michigan Public the commute consumed most of her day ([Michigan Public](https://www.michiganpublic.org/transportation-infrastructure/2026-07-27/wayne-county-voters-to-decide-fate-of-countywide-transit-in-primary-millage)).

Employers feel the same problem from the other side, which is what connects it to the "jobs and opportunity in Michigan" judging criterion. A 2024 state survey of all 16 Michigan Works! agencies found transportation was the most pressing barrier to workforce participation in every region ([Michigan LEO](https://www.michigan.gov/leo/news/2025/12/18/new-playbook-helps-employers-tackle-transportation-barriers-to-strengthen-retention-and-productivity)). A 2019 Michigan Works! Southeast study found one in four employees leaving a job over transportation conflicts while only 9% of the region's employers offered any transportation support, at a median turnover cost of about $4,000 per worker ([WHMI](https://whmi.com/news/article/34439)). Existing responses are small: the state's newest employer program awarded $60,000 across three pilots in April 2026 ([Michigan LEO](https://www.michigan.gov/leo/news/2026/04/01/three-mi-organizations-receive-grants-to-launch-employee-transportation-programs)).

Senior and disability mobility is the most serious alternative by headcount. Michigan has about 1.9 million seniors, and a Wayne State gerontologist estimates 10-15% of them struggle to find transportation ([Bridge Michigan](https://bridgemi.com/michigan-health-watch/transportation-troubles-create-lonely-reality-older-michiganders/)); roughly 1.49 million residents (14.8%) have a disability ([USAFacts](https://usafacts.org/answers/how-many-people-have-a-disability/state/michigan/)). Medicaid non-emergency rides cost $160.5 million over the 18 months to March 2026, and a follow-up state audit is due in late 2026 ([Michigan Office of the Auditor General](https://audgen.michigan.gov/?p=10993)) after the original found the system ineffective ([FOX 47 News](https://www.fox47news.com/neighborhoods/state-capitol/audit-finds-mdhhs-non-emergency-transportation-system-ineffective)). The trouble for a hackathon team is that this space is already occupied and the bottleneck is not software. Public money already backs the myride2 one-call service ([RTA](https://www.rtamichigan.org/how-to-ride/trip-planning-assistance-myride2/)), a $1.05 million RTA paratransit booking platform ([MDOT](https://www.michigan.gov/mdot/travel/mobility/initiatives/mobility-challenge/rta-paratransit-trip-management)), Feonix's assistance-hub network ([MEDC](https://www.michiganbusiness.org/press-releases/2024/10/gilchrist-announces-$15m-equitable-mobility-challenge-recipients/)) and rural trip-planning pilots ([Mass Transit](https://www.masstransitmag.com/alt-mobility/press-release/55094371/michigan-department-of-transportation-mdot-michigan-dots-advancing-rural-mobility-pilot-project-provides-new-online-tools-for-both-rural-public-transit-agencies)), yet a shared scheduling feature built into myride2 went unused by providers, many of which still dispatch from spreadsheets and index cards ([RTA OnHand plan](https://www.rtamichigan.org/app/uploads/2025/06/Appendix-D-RTA-of-EM-Strategy-Booklet.pdf)). With no booking APIs to call, a one-day build would end at an information page.

The car-cost problems are real and partly unserved. Detroit drivers pay about $4,390 a year for insurance against $2,703 statewide ([Experian](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/average-cost-car-insurance-michigan/)), and 22.3% of Michigan drivers were uninsured in 2023, fourth highest nationally ([MoneyGeek](https://www.moneygeek.com/insurance/auto/analysis/uninsured-motorist-statistics/)). Pothole damage claims almost never pay: MDOT approved 118 of 1,492 between 2018 and mid-2023 ([Bridge Michigan](https://bridgemi.com/michigan-politics/michigan-denies-almost-all-pothole-repair-claims-despite-billions-damages/)). A claims helper or license-restoration navigator is buildable with a language model and documents, but neither has live data to show, and neither maps as cleanly onto "mobility" or onto measurable Michigan jobs. These numbers work better as supporting context for the commute idea: they explain why "just buy a car" is not an answer in Detroit.

EV charging is the weakest fit despite being the obvious Mobility-track topic. Michigan had about 85,666 registered EVs in mid-2025 against a 2030 goal of 2 million ([Bridge Michigan](https://bridgemi.com/michigan-environment-watch/michigan-ev-registrations-jump-still-far-whitmers-2-million-goal/)), federal purchase credits ended in September 2025 ([NPR via Michigan Public](https://www.michiganpublic.org/2026-09-30/one-year-after-the-end-of-the-ev-tax-credit-is-the-future-still-electric)), and the state's 2026 mobility grants went mostly to drones, hydrogen and supply-chain projects, with one charging award among ten in September ([LEO/OFME](https://www.michigan.gov/leo/news/2026/09/11/whitmer-awards-funds-to-support-jobs-advance-clean-energy-technologies-and-drive-business-growth)). The charger-reliability gap is genuine, but no open source of live charger status was found, so the feature that would matter cannot be built from allowed data.

One alternative deserves respect on the Michigan-impact criterion alone: supplier workers displaced by the EV pullback. Michigan lost roughly 4,000 auto-parts jobs in the year to June 2026 ([Vehicle Service Pros](https://www.vehicleservicepros.com/aftermarket-business-world/news/news/55394515/michigan-auto-parts-sector-sheds-4000-jobs-despite-trump-tariff-claims)), and a University of Michigan report projects about 11,200 parts jobs and 4,100 upstream jobs gone by 2030, concentrated among the lowest-credential workers in smaller communities ([U-M report](https://evworkforce.umich.edu/sites/default/files/2026-07/auto-workforce-transition-report-web.pdf)). No open dataset of openings, plants or program eligibility was found to build on, which makes it a poor one-day bet, but it supplies a useful line for the commute pitch: a displaced worker's next job is often at a different plant on a different shift, and whether it is reachable decides whether the job is real.

## The evidence backs a round-trip shift check, provided "reliably" is earned

Four findings support the idea directly. The RTA study shows the failure is time-of-day specific, not just geographic. The same study recommends surveying employers on shift times, piloting a guaranteed ride home and giving job seekers better information, so the region's transit authority has effectively written the problem statement ([RTA report](https://www.rtamichigan.org/app/uploads/2025/06/RTA-Detroit-Workforce-Mobility-Equity-Report-F-1.pdf)). Job seekers in that study reported turning down jobs beyond the mileage limit of the Lyft-based Ride United program, which shows people already make accept-or-decline decisions on commute feasibility with poor information. And the state's 2025 mobility fund named first- and last-mile transit gaps as one of five problems it wanted solved ([OFME](https://www.michigan.gov/leo/bureaus-agencies/ofme/projects-and-programs/funding-and-testing/technology-deployments/arpa-mobility-fund)).

The idea has three weaknesses the team should design around before judges raise them.

The first is the word "reliably". No usable live feed exists for a one-day build: DDOT lists no public realtime feed and its director told City Council in March 2026 that app-based tracking was becoming less dependable ([Axios Detroit](https://www.axios.com/local/detroit/2026/03/17/detroit-seeks-fix-for-unreliable-bus-tracking)); SMART's realtime feed sits behind a Swiftly key that requires a reviewed application with no published turnaround ([Mobility Database](https://mobilitydatabase.org/feeds/gtfs_rt/mdb-1442)). The honest version of "reliable" is a **risk score computed from the schedule**: number of transfers, the headway on each leg (what a missed bus costs in minutes), the margin between shift end and the last trip home, and published agency on-time rates. This is a stronger product than a live-tracking claim, because a job decision depends on whether the commute holds up over months, not on where one bus is tonight. The team should state plainly that the score is schedule-based.

The second is that a verdict does not move anyone. When the answer is "no" or "risky", the tool needs to say what would close the gap. The notes identify the real options and their limits: SMART Flex microtransit runs roughly 5 or 6 a.m. to 11 p.m. in five zones ([RTA Existing Conditions memo](https://www.rtamichigan.org/app/uploads/2025/10/AppB_Existing-Conditions-Technical-Memo_508_10092025.pdf)); MichiVan vanpools start at $55 a month for 5 to 15 commuters, and the state's Guaranteed Ride Home excludes trips to work ([MDOT](https://www.michigan.gov/mdot/Travel/Commuters/ridesharing)). A second audience makes the effectiveness and Michigan-impact case stronger still: an employer or Michigan Works! counselor view that shows which shift times cut a plant off from car-less applicants, and how many more become reachable if a shift ends 30 minutes earlier. A 2016 Livingston County survey found 57 employers willing to coordinate shifts if transportation existed ([Bridge Michigan](https://bridgemi.com/business-watch/people-need-jobs-factories-need-workers-busing-love-story/)); it is old, but it suggests the employer side is an information problem as much as a funding one.

The third is differentiation. RTA partnered with the Transit app in November 2025 to cover DDOT, SMART and the People Mover in one planner ([RTA](https://www.rtamichigan.org/rta-news/what-to-know-about-app-that-can-help-commuters-plan-trips-easily-in-detroit)), LinkedIn has shown commute estimates on job listings since 2018 ([Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/linkedins-your-commute-brings-transit-time-your-job-search)), and TravelTime sells commute search to job boards ([TravelTime](https://traveltime.com/search-api/job-board)). None of the tools found checks both directions against a specific shift, returns a verdict instead of a single itinerary, or stitches in fallbacks. That gap is real, but it rests on about a dozen searches; the pitch should say "we found no tool that does this", not "none exists".

## Open schedules, Routes API and Granite tool calling cover the build

The core data is free and current. DDOT's static GTFS is a public-domain direct download valid through February 7, 2027 ([Mobility Database](https://mobilitydatabase.org/feeds/gtfs/mdb-464); [data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/ddot-gtfs-file)), and SMART's is a direct download valid through January 3, 2027 ([Mobility Database](https://mobilitydatabase.org/feeds/gtfs/mdb-414)). Google's Routes API accepts a transit request with either an arrival time or a departure time, which maps onto two calls per job: arrive by shift start, depart after shift end ([Google Routes API](https://developers.google.com/maps/documentation/routes/transit-route)). Since March 2025 Google allows 10,000 free calls a month on its Essentials tier and 5,000 on Pro, ample for a demo ([Google Maps Platform pricing](https://mapsplatform.google.com/pricing?authuser=1)). On the IBM side, watsonx.ai's chat API supports function calling with Granite ([IBM tool calling](https://www.ibm.com/watsonx/developer/capabilities/tool-calling)), and the watsonx Orchestrate developer kit wraps REST APIs as agent tools through an OpenAPI spec or a Python function ([IBM ADK](https://developer.watson-orchestrate.ibm.com/tools/overview)).

| **Need** | **Build on** | **Status and caution** |
|---|---|---|
| Transit routing by shift time | Google Routes API, transit mode | Free tier sufficient; late-night DDOT/SMART results were not tested, so run one 11 p.m. query first |
| Last-bus and headway checks | DDOT and SMART static GTFS zips | Open, no key; the dependable fallback if Routes results disappoint |
| Demo destinations | RTA study's named clusters (Jefferson North, Amazon Fairgrounds, Cadillac Stamping, hospitals) | Credible and pre-analyzed; use real addresses and shift times |
| Weather risk on walk and wait legs | National Weather Service API | Free, keyless ([NWS](https://www.weather.gov/documentation/services-web-api)) |
| Walk-to-stop safety | Detroit crash points 2011-2024, ArcGIS service | Keyless, 2,000 records per request ([City of Detroit](https://services2.arcgis.com/qvkbeam7Wirps6zC/arcgis/rest/services/Traffic_Crashes/FeatureServer)) |
| Where car-less workers' jobs are | Census LODES home-to-work files | Free, but has no shift times ([Census LEHD](https://lehd.ces.census.gov/data/)) |
| Agent and reasoning | watsonx Orchestrate ADK plus Granite | Python 3.11+, 30-day trial; confirm which Granite model IDs the event enables |
| Job listings | None open | No Pure Michigan Talent Connect API found; use pasted address and shift, or a small curated list |
| Live bus positions | Swiftly (SMART), DDOT realtime | Gated or undocumented; do not plan around them |

Three unknowns need checking on the day: whether Google returns DDOT and SMART itineraries for late-night trips, which billing tier a transit request falls under, and what credits or pre-provisioned instances the organizers supply. None of the endpoints was called during research, because the research environment blocked direct requests, so each should be hit once before the team commits to it.

Two further cautions apply. Insurance premiums differ widely between comparison sites, so any Detroit-versus-state comparison must come from one source. And the DDOT figures are ten months old: the dashboard had no data past January 2026 when read, and on-time performance swung from 82% to 68% within the prior year, so the team should check the dashboard on the day and quote whatever month it shows.

## Conclusion

The research changes the idea less than it sharpens it. The problem is not that people lack a trip planner; it is that a job offer is a months-long commitment, and nobody tells a car-less applicant whether the commute will hold up in both directions at the hours the shift demands. That reframes the product from navigation to a decision: take the job, ask for a different shift, or line up a vanpool before day one. It also supplies a second customer the notes show is underserved, the employer or workforce counselor who cannot see that an 11:30 p.m. shift end is what empties the applicant pool.

The Wayne County vote gives the pitch something rare, a dated reason this matters now. For the next three years suburban routes arrive in waves, and jobs that were unreachable in 2025 become reachable on a schedule nobody is tracking job by job. A tool that re-scores the same commute as each feed updates turns a millage expected to raise at least $570 million over ten years ([Detroit News via Mass Transit](https://www.masstransitmag.com/management/news/55403322/mi-smart-plans-for-expansion-into-new-communities-after-countywide-vote-passes)) into filled shifts, which is the most direct available answer to how a one-day build keeps jobs and opportunity in Michigan. The largest open risks are practical and cheap to close: one late-night test query, one confirmation of the Granite model IDs on offer, and a fresh read of the DDOT dashboard.
